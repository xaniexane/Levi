## Purpose
- Measure how mature the organization's cybersecurity practices are against the NIST CSF 2.0 Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover functions.
- Produce a prioritized roadmap that ties improvement actions to business risk and target maturity levels.
- Create a repeatable assessment baseline so progress can be demonstrated to leadership, auditors, and regulators year over year.

## When to use
- When leadership asks for an honest picture of the security program's strengths and gaps.
- Before building an annual security plan or budget, to justify investment with evidence.
- When a customer, partner, or regulator asks for a framework-aligned self-assessment.
- After a major incident, to identify the control failures the response exposed.

## Prerequisites
- The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 reference and a defined target profile for the organization.
- Access to policy documents, control inventories, and stakeholders across IT, security, and business units.
- A maturity scale agreed in advance (for example initial, developing, defined, managed, adaptive).
- Evidence of current practices: tickets, reports, configurations, and audit results.

## Procedure
1. Define scope and target profile: which business units, systems, and CSF categories are in scope, and what maturity the organization aims for.
2. Gather evidence for each in-scope subcategory through document review, interviews, and control sampling.
3. Score current maturity per subcategory against the agreed scale, recording the evidence behind every score.
4. Identify gaps between current and target maturity and estimate the effort and risk reduction of closing each.
5. Prioritize gaps by risk: which weaknesses would hurt most if exploited, and which improvements are cheapest to make.
6. Draft the roadmap with owners, milestones, and metrics so progress is trackable quarter by quarter.
7. Validate findings with stakeholders to catch misunderstandings before the report goes to leadership.
8. Present results with a clear current-versus-target view and the top five actions for the next year.
9. Archive the assessment package so the next cycle starts from evidence, not memory.
10. Schedule the reassessment cadence and assign ownership for tracking roadmap progress.
11. Map assessment results to cyber-insurance and customer questionnaire answers so the same evidence serves multiple asks.
12. Track improvement metrics quarterly and report progress against the target profile to keep momentum.

## Expected outputs
- A scored assessment across CSF functions and categories with evidence for each score.
- A prioritized improvement roadmap with owners, timelines, and success metrics.
- An executive summary that translates technical gaps into business risk language.

## Pitfalls
- Scoring aspirations instead of reality; every score needs evidence, not intentions.
- Assessing in a vacuum without business context, which produces technically accurate but irrelevant priorities.
- Treating the assessment as a one-time report instead of a living baseline that gets revisited.
- Letting the target profile stay aspirational forever; set realistic staged targets instead.
