# Copyright Fundamentals — Ownership and Registration

## Purpose

Answer the three questions every builder eventually faces: what does
copyright actually protect, who owns the work, and is registration worth
doing. Covers subject matter, authorship, work-for-hire, the AI-authorship
position of the Copyright Office, and the litigation gate of registration.

## The doctrine

Copyright protects "original works of authorship fixed in any tangible
medium of expression" (17 U.S.C. § 102(a)) — code, text, music, images,
audiovisual works. It protects expression, never the underlying idea,
procedure, or method of operation (§ 102(b)). Protection attaches
automatically on fixation; no notice or registration is required for the
right to exist.

- **Initial ownership** vests in the author (§ 201(a)). Joint authors each
  hold an undivided interest and may each nonexclusively license the work.
- **Work made for hire** (§ 101, § 201(b)): (1) work prepared by an
  employee within the scope of employment, or (2) specially ordered or
  commissioned work *within nine enumerated categories* with a signed
  written agreement. Software commissioned from a contractor is generally
  *not* in the enumerated categories — a contractor's code needs an
  express assignment, not just a "work for hire" label, to transfer
  ownership.
- **AI-assisted works:** the Copyright Office's position (Compendium
  § 306; 2023–2025 guidance) is that purely machine-generated output
  lacks human authorship and is not registrable; works with sufficient
  human creative control over the expressive elements may be. Applicants
  must disclose AI-generated content that is more than de minimis. This
  area is still developing — flag close cases for counsel.
- **Registration** (§§ 408–412): optional for ownership, but required
  before filing an infringement suit for US works (§ 411(a)), and timely
  registration (within 3 months of publication or before infringement)
  unlocks statutory damages and attorney's fees (§ 412) — the remedies
  that make enforcement economically viable.
- **Duration:** life of the author + 70 years for individual works; 95/120
  years for works made for hire (§ 302).

## Checklist

1. **Confirm authorship chain.** Employee → employer owns via work-for-hire
   (scope-of-employment documented). Contractor → get a signed written
   assignment of copyright, not a bare "work for hire" recital.
2. **Register valuable works early.** Group registration options exist for
   photographs, serial issues, and unpublished collections; the eCO online
   system is the standard filing path (copyright.gov/registration).
3. **Record transfers.** Assignments and exclusive licenses should be
   recorded with the Copyright Office (§ 205) to protect against
   subsequent conflicting transfers.
4. **Mark AI involvement honestly.** In registration applications,
   disclose AI-generated portions beyond de minimis; keep records of the
   human-authored expressive choices.
5. **Preserve evidence of creation.** Dated source control history is
   practical proof of independent creation and authorship order.

## Red flags

- Contractor agreement says "work for hire" but the work isn't in the
  nine statutory categories and has no assignment clause — the contractor
  still owns it.
- Assuming registration is unnecessary because "copyright is automatic"
  — true for the right, false for the lawsuit and the real remedies.
- AI-generated assets filed for registration without disclosure.
- Open-source contributions made without checking whether the
  contributor's employer claims the IP (many employment agreements do).
