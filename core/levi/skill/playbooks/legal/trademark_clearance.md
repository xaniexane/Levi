# Trademark Clearance and Registration

## Purpose

Keep a new brand (LEVI, OMEGA, Alpha, product names, dynasty marks) from
colliding with existing rights. Covers the distinctiveness spectrum,
knockout vs. full clearance search, USPTO filing mechanics, office-action
risk, and maintenance. Trademark disputes are the most common way a young
brand pays a surprise tax — clearance is cheaper than rebranding.

## The doctrine

Trademark rights in the US arise from *use in commerce*, not registration
— but federal registration (Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1051+) confers
nationwide constructive use, prima facie validity, incontestability after
five years, and access to federal courts with enhanced remedies.

- **Distinctiveness spectrum** (Abercrombie): fanciful/arbitrary
  (strongest) → suggestive → descriptive (protectable only with acquired
  secondary meaning) → generic (never protectable). "Apple" for computers
  is arbitrary; "Tasty" for food is descriptive.
- **§ 1052 bars:** the USPTO refuses marks that are likely to cause
  confusion with registered marks, merely descriptive without secondary
  meaning, deceptive, disparaging-adjacent (post-*Matal v. Tam* /
  *Iancu v. Brunetti* the disparagement/immoral-scandalous bars are gone),
  or functional trade dress.
- **Likelihood of confusion** is the infringement test (§ 1114 for
  registered marks, § 1125(a) for unregistered): similarity of marks,
  relatedness of goods/services, channels of trade, consumer
  sophistication — the multi-factor tests (e.g., DuPont factors at the
  TTAB) are fact-heavy.
- **Use-based (§ 1(a)) vs. intent-to-use (§ 1(b)) filing:** § 1(b) lets
  you lock a filing date before launch, but registration requires a later
  statement of use with specimens.
- **Maintenance:** §§ 8/9 declarations and renewals (5–6 year, then
  every 10); deadwood cancellations happen — a registration on the
  register is not proof of live use.

## Checklist

1. **Knockout search first.** USPTO TESS/TM Search
   (tmsearch.uspto.gov) for identical/near-identical marks in related
   classes; web search for common-law uses (unregistered users have
   enforceable regional rights).
2. **Full clearance before money is spent.** A professional search covers
   state registries, business names, domains, and common-law sources; for
   a flagship brand this is not optional.
3. **Pick the right classes.** File in the International Classes covering
   actual/near-term goods and services; overclaiming invites challenges.
4. **Choose filing basis.** Already in interstate commerce → § 1(a) with
   specimens. Pre-launch → § 1(b) intent-to-use, calendar the statement
   of use deadlines (extensions available, finite).
5. **Watch the application.** Office actions (descriptiveness, confusion
   refusals) are routine — respond on deadline; publication triggers a
   30-day opposition window (TTAB).
6. **Calendar maintenance.** § 8 declaration at 5–6 years, § 9 renewal
   every 10; docket it like a tax deadline.

## Red flags

- Launching a brand on a TESS knockout search alone for a flagship mark.
- Descriptive names filed without evidence of secondary meaning.
- Filing in classes you will never use — vulnerability to
  nonuse cancellation.
- Assuming a domain registration or LLC name equals trademark rights.
- Ignoring common-law users: a senior regional user can enjoin you in
  their territory despite your later federal registration.
